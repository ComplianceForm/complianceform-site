Fire alarm log book: what to record and how to test

Updated 9 November 2026

A fire alarm log book is the written history of your fire alarm system. Every weekly test, every service visit, every fault and every false alarm goes in it, with the date and the name of whoever dealt with it. If the alarm ever fails to do its job, or a fire officer asks how the system is looked after, the log book is how you show what has been happening.

This guide covers what a fire alarm log book should contain, how the weekly test works in practice and how long it's sensible to keep the records.

Do you have to keep one?

In England and Wales, the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 puts the responsible person under a duty to keep fire safety systems maintained in efficient working order. The Order doesn't name a log book as such, but it is very difficult to show a system has been maintained without a written record, and fire and rescue services routinely ask to see one during audits.

The British Standard for fire detection and alarm systems in non-domestic premises, BS 5839-1, expects a log book to be kept and sets out the routine testing and servicing that should be recorded in it. Insurers often ask for the same evidence after a claim.

So while the law talks about outcomes rather than paperwork, in practice a log book is expected.

What goes in a fire alarm log book

A useful log book has a short front section about the system and then a running record of events. The front section should cover:

  • the name of the responsible person and whoever looks after the alarm day to day
  • the company that services the system, with contact details
  • where the control panel is and what type of system it is
  • a zone plan or list of call points, so testers know which one to use next

The running record should then capture:

EventWhat to write down
Weekly testDate, time, which call point was used, whether the alarm was heard everywhere, whether the panel reset normally, who tested it
Service visitDate, company, engineer, work done, any defects found, certificate or report reference
FaultWhen it was noticed, what the panel showed, who was told, when it was fixed
False alarmDate, time, device that triggered, likely cause, action taken to stop it happening again
AlterationsAny devices added, moved or removed, and who did the work

How the weekly test works

BS 5839-1 recommends a test every week, using a different manual call point each time so that, over a cycle, every call point gets used. Most call points have a test key that triggers the alarm without breaking the glass element.

Pick a regular time so people learn that a short alarm at, say, ten o'clock on a Monday is the test and not a real fire. Before you start, tell anyone who needs to know, including the alarm receiving centre if the system is monitored. Then:

  1. Activate the next call point in your rotation with the test key.
  2. Check the sounders operate and can be heard throughout the building. A quick word with someone at the far end helps.
  3. Reset the call point and the panel, and confirm the panel shows normal.
  4. Write it down straight away: date, time, call point, result and your name.

If anything doesn't work, the alarm doesn't sound in part of the building, or the panel won't reset, record it as a fault and contact your service company. Don't just note it and move on.

Servicing

The weekly test only proves the basics. A competent person should also service the system periodically, and BS 5839-1 recommends that service visits are no more than six months apart. The engineer should leave a certificate or report. Note the visit in the log book and keep the paperwork with it.

False alarms deserve attention

It is tempting to treat a false alarm as a nuisance and forget it, but they belong in the log too. A pattern of false alarms from the same detector or zone is often the first sign of a problem, and too many of them teach people to ignore the sound. Recording the device and the probable cause each time lets you, and your service company, spot the pattern.

How long to keep the records

There is no single fixed retention period written into fire safety law for alarm records. A sensible approach is to keep the log book and service reports for the life of the system and at least until well after your next fire risk assessment review. That way you can always show a continuous history. Storing a scanned or spreadsheet copy as well as the paper original protects you if the book goes missing.

Paper or spreadsheet?

A paper log book kept beside the panel has the advantage of being where the tester is. The weakness is that it can't tell you when something has been missed. A spreadsheet can. If you record tests in a spreadsheet that works out the next due date, a missed week shows up immediately rather than at the next audit.

Many sites use both: a paper sheet by the panel for the person testing, typed into the spreadsheet afterwards by whoever manages the building.
