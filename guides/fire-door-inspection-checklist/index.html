Fire door inspection checklist: the five checks and how often

Updated 9 November 2026

A fire door only works if it is shut, fits its frame properly and hasn't been damaged. Doors get kicked, propped open, painted over and fitted with new locks, so they need checking regularly. This guide explains who has to check fire doors, how often, and the five things to look at each time.

Who has to check fire doors, and how often

In England, regulation 10 of the Fire Safety (England) Regulations 2022 came into force on 23 January 2023. For multi-occupied residential buildings with storeys over 11 metres in height, the responsible person must:

  • check all fire doors in the communal parts, including their self-closing devices, at least every three months
  • use best endeavours to check every flat entrance door that opens onto the communal parts, including its self-closing device, at least once a year

Lift landing doors are not included in these checks.

For buildings of 11 metres or less, these specific intervals don't apply. The responsible person still has a general duty under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 to make sure fire doors, including flat entrance doors, give adequate protection, so regular checks remain sensible. Residential buildings with two or more sets of domestic premises must also give residents information about fire doors, whatever their height.

Do you need a specialist?

Not normally. The Home Office fact sheet on regulation 10 describes these checks as looking for obvious damage or problems, and says the responsible person should usually be able to carry them out. If a check turns up damage or something you are unsure about, that is the time to bring in someone with specialist fire door knowledge.

The five-point fire door checklist

The government fact sheet lists what a responsible person should consider. Turned into a checklist, it looks like this:

CheckWhat a problem looks like
1. Glazing and air transfer grillesCracked or loose glass, missing beading, a grille that has been altered or damaged
2. Gaps, seals and hingesLarge or uneven gaps around the frame, missing or painted-over intumescent strips or smoke seals, loose or missing hinge screws
3. The closer shuts the doorOpen the door fully and let go. It should close by itself without help
4. The door closes properly into the frameIt catches on the floor or frame, bounces back or stops short of the latch
5. Visible damageHoles, splits, kick damage, removed or replaced parts, damage to the closer

If a door fails any one of these, record it as a defect, decide how urgent it is and set a date for the repair. A door that won't close or has a hole in it should be dealt with quickly.

Flat entrance doors and "best endeavours"

You can't always get into a flat to check its front door. The regulations recognise this by asking for best endeavours. If a resident won't give access, keep evidence of what you tried, such as letters, emails and notes of appointments offered. The Home Office fact sheet specifically suggests keeping this kind of correspondence.

Recording the checks

The fact sheet doesn't prescribe a format for records, but you'll want to be able to show which doors were checked, when, by whom, and what was found. A practical approach is:

  1. Give every fire door a reference (FD01, FD02 and so on) and list them all in a register with their location and whether they are communal or flat entrance doors.
  2. Each time you check a door, record the date, the reference, the answer to each of the five points and your name.
  3. Copy any failures onto a defects list with a target date, and close them off when fixed.

Kept this way, your records show at a glance which doors are due, which are overdue and which have outstanding repairs.
