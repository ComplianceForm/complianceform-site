Emergency lighting log book: monthly and annual tests explained

Updated 9 November 2026

Emergency lighting has one job, to light the way out when the mains supply fails, and it is only ever called on at the worst possible moment. That's why it needs regular testing, and why the tests need writing down. An emergency lighting log book is where that record lives.

Here is what to test, how often, and what your log book should show.

The two tests: monthly and annual

The code of practice for emergency lighting, BS 5266-1, sets out two routine tests.

Monthly function test

Once a month, every emergency light fitting is switched to battery by simulating a mains failure, usually with a test key switch. The point is to check that each fitting lights up and that any exit signs are illuminated. It only needs to run long enough to confirm that, so it takes a minute or two rather than hours.

Walk the whole building while the test is running. It is easy to check the corridor outside the plant room and forget the fitting at the back of the store.

Annual full duration test

Once a year, the system is run on battery for its full rated duration. That is commonly three hours, though some systems are rated for one hour. Your installation certificate will say which. At the end of the test every fitting should still be lit. A fitting that has dimmed or gone out has a battery that can no longer do its job.

This test is normally carried out by a competent person, often an electrical contractor, who should issue a certificate. Because the batteries can take up to a day to recharge afterwards, the test is best done when the building is quiet or empty, or by testing alternate fittings so that some cover always remains.

What to record

ItemWhy it matters
Date and type of testShows the monthly and annual cycle is being kept up
Which fittings were testedProves the whole building was covered, not just the easy bits
How long the test ranSeparates a quick function test from a full duration test
Any fittings that failed, by referenceLets you chase the repair and check the same fitting next time
Action takenShows defects were dealt with, not just noticed
Name of the testerAccountability, and someone to ask if questions come up later

It helps to give every fitting a short reference such as EL1, EL2 and so on, marked on a simple floor plan. Then a failure can be written as "EL7 did not light" rather than "the one near the kitchen", and anyone can find it.

When a fitting fails

A failed fitting is a gap in your escape lighting until it is repaired. Record it, report it to whoever maintains the system and give it a target date. When it is fixed, record that too. If the same fitting fails repeatedly, mention it to your contractor, as it may point to a wiring or supply problem rather than just a tired battery.

Self-testing systems

Some newer installations test themselves automatically and report the results to a panel or software. They save a lot of walking about, but someone still needs to check the results, act on failures and keep a record. A self-test that nobody reads is no better than a test that never happened.

Keeping the records together

Keep the installation certificate, the annual test certificates and your monthly records together, whether that is in a ring binder, a spreadsheet or both. A fire officer, insurer or auditor will want to see the full picture: what was installed, how it is tested and what happened when something failed.
